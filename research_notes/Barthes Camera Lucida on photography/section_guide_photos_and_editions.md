## 1. What each of the 48 sections does (with key lines, and where the main concepts come in)

### Takeaway
The book has 48 short numbered sections in two parts of 24 each. Part One (§§1–24) builds and then takes back a "casual phenomenology" of the Spectator: Operator/Spectator/Spectrum in §4, studium/punctum in §10, the "unary" photograph in §17, the palinode in §24. Part Two (§§25–48) starts from his mother's death and the Winter Garden photograph (§28) and arrives at the noeme "That-has-been" (§32), Time as punctum (§39), "camera lucida" (§44) and the "tamed" photograph (§48). The French edition has a table of contents giving each section a title. A full sourced summary of every section could not be assembled online in this session (see Gaps). An unverified working table for the writer to check is in the Gaps section.

### Cited Findings
- **Structure:** 48 chapters in two parts. French Wikipedia: "se compose de 48 chapitres divisés en deux parties" — [fr.wikipedia, La Chambre claire](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Chambre_claire). Another source says each part has 24 short chapters — [fr.wikipedia via search](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Chambre_claire); Batchen also discusses the two-part, twenty-four-section form — [Geoffrey Batchen, "Judging a Book by Its Cover", Cabinet 36, Winter 2009–10](https://www.cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php).
- **Writing and printing dates:** written April–June 1979 and published by Gallimard in February 1980 — [Jacques Ould Aoudia reading note](https://jacques-ould-aoudia.net/la-chambre-claire-roland-barthes-note-de-lecture/). Printing finished on 25 January 1980; it was Barthes's last book, out weeks before his death — [Érudit / 24 images review (PDF)](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/images/2016-n177-images02511/81944ac.pdf).
- **The French table of contents gives section titles.** Google Books' record of the French edition lists "Spécialité de la Photo · 13", "La Photo inclassable · 14", "L'émotion comme départ · 20" (§§1–3 with French page numbers), followed by "Operator, Spectrum et Spectator" — [Google Books, La chambre claire](https://books.google.de/books/about/La_chambre_claire.html?id=l4wHAQAAIAAJ&redir_esc=y); [livres-cinema.info](https://www.livres-cinema.info/livre/11923/chambre-claire).
- **Late Part One titles in order:** "Punctum : trait partiel", "Co-présence du Studium et du Punctum", "Trait involontaire", "Satori", "Après coup et silence", "Champ aveugle", "Palinodie" — [livres-cinema.info](https://www.livres-cinema.info/livre/11923/chambre-claire). (The search summary put "Co-présence" after "Punctum : trait partiel". Background knowledge says the order is §18 Co-présence, then §19 Punctum : trait partiel. Check this.) The same page lists "La Photographie du Jardin d'Hiver" and "La pose" in Part Two — [livres-cinema.info](https://www.livres-cinema.info/livre/11923/chambre-claire).
- **§1:** opens with Barthes coming across an 1852 photograph of Jérôme, Napoleon's youngest brother, and being struck that he was looking at eyes that had looked at the Emperor. This prompts the question of what photography is "in itself" — [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/camera-lucida/summary/); [MIT photo course blog, "A not-so-short summary"](https://mitphoto2016.wordpress.com/2016/04/29/a-not-so-short-summary-of-camera-lucida/); [Art History Unstuffed](https://arthistoryunstuffed.com/roland-barthes-camera-lucida/).
- **§2 ("The Photograph Unclassifiable" / "La Photo inclassable"):** photography resists the usual classifications (professional/amateur, genre and so on) — [Course Hero section summaries](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/summaries/); [mystudies résumé](https://www.mystudies.com/fr-fr/matieres-artistiques-et-mediatiques/arts/fiche-de-lecture/roland-barthes-la-chambre-claire-resume-858161.html).
- **§4 (concept introduced: Operator / Spectator / Spectrum):** the Operator is the photographer, the Spectator the viewer, the Spectrum the person or thing photographed (the referent) — [MIT photo course blog](https://mitphoto2016.wordpress.com/2016/04/29/a-not-so-short-summary-of-camera-lucida/); [Course Hero](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/summaries/).
- **§§9–10 (concepts introduced: duality, then studium / punctum):** a Koen Wessing photograph (Nicaragua, 1979) "animates" Barthes through the incongruous nuns in the background. This duality leads to the naming of studium (general, culturally coded interest) and punctum (the detail that pricks). Course Hero treats "Chapters 9–10" as the place where these terms are introduced — [Course Hero](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/summaries/); [Course Hero plot summary](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/plot-summary/). The punctum passage is widely quoted as the detail that "rises from the scene, shoots out of it like an arrow, and pierces me" — [MIT photo course blog](https://mitphoto2016.wordpress.com/2016/04/29/a-not-so-short-summary-of-camera-lucida/).
- **§15 (the "mask"):** Barthes uses Avedon's portrait of William Casby (born a slave) as a mask that lays bare the essence of slavery — [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/camera-lucida/summary/).
- **§17 (concept introduced: unary photograph):** a photograph whose studium is not disturbed by any punctum is "unary", that is, banal — [MIT photo course blog](https://mitphoto2016.wordpress.com/2016/04/29/a-not-so-short-summary-of-camera-lucida/).
- **§§19–22 (the punctum as detail):** examples include the strapped pumps in Van Der Zee's 1926 family portrait, a child's bad teeth in a William Klein photograph, and the dirt road in a Kertész photograph of a blind Gypsy violinist, which Barthes ties to villages he passed through in Hungary and Romania. The punctum must not be intentional — [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/camera-lucida/summary/); [Kirkus Reviews](https://kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/roland-barthes/camera-lucida/print). In §22 the punctum "after the fact": Barthes revises the Van Der Zee punctum from the shoes to the woman's necklace, because the image had "worked within" him. The necklace passage is around p. 53 of the English — [Media-studies.com](https://media-studies.com/studium-and-punctum/); [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/camera-lucida/summary/).
- **Part Two page anchors (Cape 1982 / Howard pagination):** "Part II: Section 25 (page 63)"; "67: beginning of section 28" — [Andrew King, Univ. of Greenwich, CL Study Guide, Annotations 2](https://blogs.gre.ac.uk/andrewking/2013/03/07/roland-barthes-camera-lucida-a-study-guide-2/).
- **§§25–30 (the mother):** after her death Barthes sorts her photographs. Most show her likeness but not her being, until he finds the "true" image: a picture of her as a small child, the Winter Garden photograph, which he does not reproduce. Course Hero handles these in pairs (27–28, 29–30) — [Course Hero, Part 2 Ch. 27–28](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/part-2-chapters-27-28-summary/); [Course Hero, Part 2 Ch. 29–30](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/part-2-chapters-29-30-summary/). "Ariadne" (§30): the photograph as the thread through the labyrinth, after Nietzsche's line about the labyrinth man seeking his Ariadne (student-blog reading) — [MIT photo course blog, "Barthes: Camera Lucida"](https://mitphoto2016.wordpress.com/2016/11/15/barthes-camera-lucida/).
- **§32 (concept introduced: "That-has-been" / "Ça a été", the noeme):** the photograph attests that its referent has been; this makes every image melancholy — [Course Hero plot summary](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/plot-summary/); [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/camera-lucida/summary/). The Japanese edition's blurb names the noeme "それは-かつて-あった" — [artscape.jp](https://artscape.jp/artword/5473/).
- **§§31–48 (survey):** Barthes's mother was more than a "Mother figure"; he prefers black-and-white because colour is an artifice; the punctum takes more than one form; he calls a person's essence "the air", which a photograph can sometimes catch; a final pair of sections draws the threads together. Course Hero summarises §§37–48 in pairs, including the "Totality-of-Image" and the futility of scrutinising (enlarging) photographs to get more meaning out of them — [Course Hero section summaries](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/summaries/).
- **§39 (concept introduced: Time as punctum):** under Gardner's 1865 portrait of Lewis Payne: "He is dead and he is going to die." "The photograph is handsome, as is the boy: that is the studium. But the punctum is: he is going to die." Barthes reads "this will be" and "this has been" at once. A blog attributes the line to §39 — [Art Blart](https://artblart.com/tag/hot-dead-guys-lewis-powell/); [PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2013/10/25/assassin-camera/); [The Frailest Thing](https://thefrailestthing.com/tag/civil-war/).

### Inferences
- §10 is the standard place to cite the first definitions of studium and punctum, §32 for "That-has-been", and §39 for the second, temporal punctum. The cited summaries all put the concepts in these sections, and Course Hero's pairing (9–10) agrees.
- The English translation seems not to print the French section titles (background; check). Study guides such as Course Hero appear to supply English titles of their own ("The Photograph Unclassifiable"). Cite sections by number, not by guide-supplied title.

## 2. Every photograph reproduced: photographer, subject, date, section, and what Barthes picks out

### Takeaway
The French edition reproduces 25 images: a colour Polaroid frontispiece by Daniel Boudinet that Barthes never discusses, plus 24 black-and-white photographs, mostly taken from close at hand, above all the November 1977 *Nouvel Observateur* photography issue. French Wikipedia gives the full list of photographers with page numbers in CC. The Winter Garden photograph is deliberately left out. Matching each image to its section and punctum could be done reliably for about half of them; the rest are flagged.

### Cited Findings
- **Number of images:** "Le livre est illustré par 25 photographies, anciennes et contemporaines, choisies par l'écrivain" — [fr.wikipedia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Chambre_claire); [Encyclopædia Universalis](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/la-chambre-claire/). Batchen: "Barthes makes frequent references to particular photographs in his book but only twenty-four of these are illustrated. Reproduced in black and white, they were mostly drawn from sources close at hand, and particularly from that issue of *Le Nouvel Observateur*" (the November 1977 issue carrying a French translation of Benjamin's "Little History of Photography") — [Batchen, Cabinet 36](https://www.cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php). Note: 24 black-and-white images plus 1 colour frontispiece = 25, which reconciles the two counts.
- **Frontispiece:** Daniel Boudinet, *Polaroïd*, 1979. "The monochrome blue-green photograph of a diaphanous curtain is the most recent, and only color, image to appear in the book. Barthes never discusses it in the text." Barthes first saw it at the opening of a Boudinet exhibition in April 1979, while still writing — [Batchen, Cabinet 36](https://www.cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php). English editions also print it in colour: "in color, (Daniel Boudinet, polaroid, 1979), b & w plates" (US) — [bookseller listing via search](https://www.abebooks.com/Camera-Lucida-Reflections-Photography-Barthes-Roland/32362928035/bd); "colour frontispiece" (Cape 1982) — [ZVAB listing](https://www.zvab.com/buch-suchen/isbn/9780224029292/).
- **French Wikipedia "Corpus", in order of appearance with CC page numbers:** Daniel Boudinet (p. 11); Alfred Stieglitz (35); Koen Wessing (43, 45); William Klein (53, 76); Richard Avedon (62, 170); August Sander (64); Charles Clifford (67); James Van der Zee (75); André Kertész (78, 132, 173, 174); Lewis Hine (83); Nadar (85, 108, 158); Robert Mapplethorpe (86, 94); G. W. Wilson (92); Nicéphore Niépce (137); Alexander Gardner (149); one anonymous image (163) — [fr.wikipedia, La Chambre claire, "Corpus"](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Chambre_claire).   - **Koen Wessing, Nicaragua, 1979** (two images, CC pp. 43, 45; §§9–10): the incongruous nuns behind the soldiers show the "duality" that leads to studium/punctum — [Course Hero](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/summaries/).

(The Philadelphia Museum holds Klein's *Street Dance, May Day, Moscow*, 1959, but no source tied it to the book — [PMA](https://www.philamuseum.org/objects/145585).)

  - **Charles Clifford, *The Alhambra (Granada)*** (CC p. 67; §16): Barthes: "this old photograph (1854) touches me"; landscape he wants to live in. Date conflict: 1854 (blog quoting the book; essay) vs "1854–56"/"1954–56" (study list; 1954 is an obvious typo) — [search summary of sources incl. studylib](https://studylib.net/doc/5225214/roland-barthes-camera-lucida).
  - **James Van Der Zee, *Family Portrait*, 1926** (CC p. 75; §19 then §22): studium = the respectable dress of a Black American family; first punctum = "the strapped pumps" (and the low-slung belt), then corrected to the necklace, which recalls one worn by a woman in his own family — [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/camera-lucida/summary/); [Media-studies.com](https://media-studies.com/studium-and-punctum/).

## 5. Study aids

### Takeaway
The best free aid is Andrew King's three-part University of Greenwich study guide, which goes section by section with questions and is keyed to the Cape pagination. Add the Chicago School of Media Theory annotations and the CUNY/MIT course blogs. The standard scholarly companion is Batchen (ed.), *Photography Degree Zero* (MIT Press, 2009). Commercial guides (Course Hero, SuperSummary) are partly paywalled.

### Cited Findings
- **Andrew King (Univ. of Greenwich), "Roland Barthes, Camera Lucida: a Study Guide", 2013, in three posts:**
  - "Before Reading the text", which presents the notes as a reading guide, not a summary
