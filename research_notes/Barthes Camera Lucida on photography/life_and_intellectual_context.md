## 1. Henriette Barthes: who she was, her death, the relationship, and the *Mourning Diary*

### Takeaway
Henriette Barthes (née Binger, born 1893) died on 25 October 1977. Barthes began the slips of paper later published as *Mourning Diary* the next day, 26 October 1977, and kept them up until 15 September 1979. The diary records an actual session with her photographs in June 1978 that seems to be the source of the "Winter Garden Photograph" episode. *Camera Lucida* moves that episode to "one November evening" just after her death, which is a documented discrepancy between the two texts.

### Cited Findings
- **Identity and dates.** Henriette Barthes, born Henriette Binger in 1893, died in 1977. "She was no writer but the mother of one" — [Michigan Quarterly Review, "The Private Art on Roland Barthes's Mourning Diary" (2014)](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/mqr/2014/06/the-private-art-on-roland-barthess-mourning-diary/). The birth year 1893 also appears in [libellus.over-blog (French blog)](https://libellus.over-blog.com/article-30425870.html). It matches *Camera Lucida*'s statement that she was five in 1898 (see `book_text_and_concepts.md`).
- **Date of death and start of the diary.** She died on 25 October 1977, and the diary begins on 26 October, the night after her death — [Publishers Weekly listing, *Mourning Diary: October 26, 1977–September 15, 1979*](https://publishersweekly.com/9780809062331); [complete-review.com](https://complete-review.com/reviews/divlitnf/barthes.htm).
- **Family background.** Barthes's father, the naval officer Louis Barthes, died in the First World War, and Henriette brought Roland up in Urt and Bayonne — [Wikipedia, "Roland Barthes"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roland_Barthes) (via search summary). Michel Salzedo was Barthes's half-brother — named as such in a search snippet from the biography-related results (the snippet's exact source page is uncertain; it is either [Marie Gil, "Life as a Text"](https://sites.cardiff.ac.uk/barthes/files/2015/11/GIL-Life-as-a-Text.pdf) or [the Nottingham repository record on Stafford](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/765179)). He was with Barthes during the June 1978 photograph session (see the diary finding below).
- **How close they were (biographical evidence).** Samoyault's biography reproduces a visa application for a trip to Romania that names Barthes's "wife". The "wife" was in fact his mother — [Philosophy in Review, review of Samoyault, *Barthes: A Biography*](https://journals.uvic.ca/index.php/pir/article/view/17086/7553). The publisher's blurb for Samoyault says he "enjoyed his mother's unfailing love" — [search summary of Polity/PW listing](https://publishersweekly.com/9781509505654).
- **The diary as material object.** According to the editor, Nathalie Léger, Barthes wrote the entries in ink, sometimes in pencil, on slips of paper (ordinary typing paper cut into quarters) that he kept in supply on his desk — [complete-review.com](https://complete-review.com/reviews/divlitnf/barthes.htm).
- **Publication.** Nathalie Léger compiled and annotated the French edition (*Journal de deuil*, 2009). Richard Howard translated it into English as *Mourning Diary* and wrote an afterword that recalls his own experience of Henriette — [search summary of complete-review.com and PW](https://complete-review.com/reviews/divlitnf/barthes.htm). The US edition is Hill and Wang (ISBN 9780809062331) — [Publishers Weekly](https://publishersweekly.com/9780809062331). A UK edition, *The Mourning Diary*, "Introduced by Michael Wood" (ISBN 9781907903106), is listed at [Blackwell's](https://blackwells.co.uk/bookshop/product/The-Mourning-Diary-by-Roland-Barthes-Nathalie-Lger-Richard-Howard/9781907903106) and [AbeBooks](https://www.abebooks.com/9781907903106/Mourning-Diary-Introduced-Michael-Wood-1907903100/plp). A library copy is catalogued as *Mourning diary: October 26, 1977–September 15, 1979* — [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/mourningdiaryoct0000bart).
- **Sources disagree on the date range.** PW and the edition's own subtitle give 26 October 1977 to 15 September 1979. complete-review.com gives 26 October 1977 to 21 June 1978 — [PW](https://publishersweekly.com/9780809062331); [complete-review.com](https://complete-review.com/reviews/divlitnf/barthes.htm). The later date is the subtitle of the published book, so it is the safer one. The shorter range may describe only the main sequence of the diary.
- **The diary and the Winter Garden photograph (important discrepancy).** A diary entry describes a photograph of his mother as a little girl beside her brother Philippe Binger, labelled "the Winter Garden of Chennevières, 1898". Commentators date the entry 13 June 1978. They note that the first reference to looking at "her photographs" is dated 11 June 1978, "not November of the previous year as he claims in Camera Lucida", and that he was not alone but with his brother Michel. One source quotes the same passage under 21 June. Commentators also take the entry as ending speculation that the Winter Garden photograph never existed — [search-result set including Amy Kenyon, "here she was" (Substack)](https://amykenyon.substack.com/p/here-she-was); [*Between* journal, "À la recherche d'une photographie perdue"](https://ojs.unica.it/index.php/between/article/download/1579/1585/); [Neil Badmington, *The Afterlives of Roland Barthes* (Bloomsbury), via dokumen.pub](https://dokumen.pub/the-afterlives-of-roland-barthes-9781474297455-9781474297486-9781474297479.html). The search summary did not say which of these pages each detail came from.
- **How *Camera Lucida* tells the same scene.** Part Two opens with Barthes going through photographs "one November evening, shortly after my mother's death". In the Winter Garden section he says "my mother was five at the time (1898)" — [Elsa Dorfman, review of *Camera Lucida*](http://archive.elsadorfman.com/barthes.htm); [Course Hero summary of Part 2, chs. 27–28](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/part-2-chapters-27-28-summary/). Wording VERIFIED in fragments (Howard trans.).
- **Scholarly reading of the diary alongside Proust.** Jennifer Rushworth (UCL) has an article on mourning, "intermittence" and Proust in Barthes — [Rushworth, "Mourning, Intermittence, Proust" (author's accepted manuscript, UCL Discovery)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10047894/1/Rushworth_Mourning_Intermittence_Proust_AAM.pdf). I could not read its content.

### Inferences
- *Camera Lucida* compresses and stages events. The diary puts the photograph session about seven and a half months after the death (June 1978) and with Michel present. The book places it "shortly after" the death, "one November evening", with Barthes alone. This fits reading the book as partly a literary construction (the "novel" Barthes was preparing; see section 2) and not a straight record.
- The diary's last date (15 September 1979) falls after *Camera Lucida* was finished (3 June 1979, per `book_text_and_concepts.md`). So the mourning notes and the book overlapped for the whole writing period. The book does not close the mourning.

- I could not confirm the year of the Hill and Wang *Mourning Diary* (widely given as 2010) from a primary page. The PW ISBN listing does not show the year in the snippet.

## 2. What Barthes was doing in 1977–1980: Collège de France, lecture courses, "Vita Nova", the commission, the accident and death

### Takeaway
Barthes gave his inaugural lecture at the Collège de France (chair of literary semiology) on 7 January 1977, nine months before his mother died. His courses moved from *How to Live Together* (1977) and *The Neutral* (1978) to *The Preparation of the Novel* (1978–80). That last course was organised around a "literary conversion" he dated to 15 April 1978 in Casablanca and a planned novel called *Vita Nova*, never written. *Camera Lucida* was written in the middle of this (15 April to 3 June 1979) on commission from Jean Narboni of *Cahiers du cinéma*. Barthes was hit by a laundry van on 25 February 1980, weeks after the book appeared, and died on 26 March 1980.

### Cited Findings
- **Chair and inaugural lecture.** Barthes was named professor at the Collège de France in 1976 and held the chair of semiology (literary semiology) from 1977 to 1980. He gave the inaugural lecture on 7 January 1977. It was published by Seuil in 1978 as *Leçon* and reissued by Points in 2015 — [Amazon.fr, *Leçon: Leçon inaugurale de la chaire de sémiologie littéraire au Collège de France prononcée le 7 janvier 1977*](https://www.amazon.fr/Le%C3%A7on-Le%C3%A7on-inaugurale-chaire-s%C3%A9miologie/dp/2757850164); [Éditions Points](https://www.editionspoints.com/ouvrage/lecon-roland-barthes/9782757850169). The lecture contains the line that language is "neither reactionary nor progressive", but "fascist" — [Goodreads video page (search summary)](https://www.goodreads.com/videos/130377-fr-le-on-inaugurale-s-miologie-litt-raire-au-coll-ge-de-france-1977).
- ***How to Live Together*.** Published in English by Columbia University Press — [Columbia UP, *How to Live Together*](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/how-to-live-together/9780231136167/).
- ***The Preparation of the Novel*.** Columbia UP published the English edition (*Lecture Courses and Seminars at the Collège de France, 1978–1979 and 1979–1980*), translated by Kate Briggs, in 2010 (512 pp.). The lectures ended "just weeks before his death" and stage a pedagogical experiment in which he combined teaching and writing to "simulate" the attempt to write a novel. The volume includes eight plans for the unwritten novel he called *Vita Nova*, and lecture notes on photography. A later French edition exists and is discussed by Kate Briggs — [Briggs, "On the New Edition of Roland Barthes's *La Préparation du roman*", *Barthes Studies*](http://sites.cardiff.ac.uk/barthes/files/2021/11/BRIGGS-Augmentation-infinie.pdf).
- **The "conversion" of 15 April 1978.** Barthes dates a "literary conversion" to 15 April 1978 in Casablanca ("La chose est datée à la façon d'une conversion : cela se passe le 15 avril 1978. - **"Longtemps, je me suis couché de bonne heure" (Proust lecture).** Given at the Collège de France on 19 October 1978, about a year after his mother's death, and repeated in New York in November 1978 as "Proust et moi". In it Barthes said he wanted to write a novel — [Diana Knight, "À quoi donc la vita nova de Barthes peut-elle nous convertir ?", *Revue Roland Barthes* (2018)](https://revue.roland-barthes.org/2018/diana-knight/a-quoi-donc-la-vita-nova-de-barthes-peut-elle-nous-convertir/); [Jennifer Rushworth, "Barthes's Return to Michelet and the Question of New Life" (UCL)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10162683/2/Rushworth_Barthes%20Return%20to%20Michelet%20for%20RS.pdf). - **Accident.** On 25 February 1980 a laundry van struck Barthes as he crossed the rue des Écoles in front of the Collège de France — [Washington Post (2017)](https://www.washingtonpost.com/entertainment/books/who-killed-roland-barthes-maybe-umberto-eco-has-a-clue/2017/08/22/6f644c8e-8696-11e7-a94f-3139abce39f5_story.html); [Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1384238). He had just had lunch with François Mitterrand, then a presidential candidate, who "had hosted a table of writers and intellectuals" — [languesensible (French Substack)](https://languesensible.substack.com/p/roland-barthes-le-deuil-comme-experience); [Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/entertainment/books/who-killed-roland-barthes-maybe-umberto-eco-has-a-clue/2017/08/22/6f644c8e-8696-11e7-a94f-3139abce39f5_story.html). Search summaries add that he carried no identification and the hospital did not know who he was for hours. Samoyault's biography opens with a prologue on the accident — [Andrew Gallix, *Literary Review*](https://literaryreview.co.uk/read-all-about-it-3).
- **Death.** He was taken to the Pitié-Salpêtrière and died on 26 March 1980, of pulmonary complications according to one source — [Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/entertainment/books/who-killed-roland-barthes-maybe-umberto-eco-has-a-clue/2017/08/22/6f644c8e-8696-11e7-a94f-3139abce39f5_story.html); [fr-academic (copy of French Wikipedia)](https://fr-academic.com/dic.nsf/frwiki/184851). Universalis attributes the death to a hospital-acquired infection ("infection nosocomiale") — [Universalis](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/la-chambre-claire/). **Disagreement:** a few sources give 25 March. 26 March is the better-supported date.

### Inferences
- *Camera Lucida* reads best as a by-product of the *Preparation of the Novel* project. The book is Barthes's most sustained piece of first-person, autobiographical writing about his mother, written during the very year (1978–79) he was lecturing on how to turn the mother's death into a "new life" of writing. The course volume's own photography notes (Columbia UP) support this connection.
- **Date coincidence (my observation; no source found that makes it):** Barthes began *Camera Lucida* on 15 April 1979, exactly one year after the dated Casablanca "conversion" of 15 April 1978. If it was deliberate, this would tie the book to the *Vita Nova* project. Treat it as an observation to check, not a finding.
- Barthes lived only about two months after publication, so the book took on the role of a last testament in its reception. That belongs to the reception researcher, but the biographical timeline is what makes such readings possible.

## 3. Barthes's earlier writing on photography and images, and how *Camera Lucida* departs from it

### Takeaway
Before 1980 Barthes treated photography mainly as a semiotic and ideological object. *Mythologies* (1957) dismantled the myths in photographs: the "Family of Man" exhibition, election portraits, "shock-photos". The 1960s essays ("The Photographic Message", 1961; "Rhetoric of the Image", 1964) analysed press and advertising images as coded messages. "The Third Meaning" (1970) already pointed to a meaning in film stills that escapes the code, and scholars see it as the forerunner of the punctum. *Roland Barthes by Roland Barthes* (1975) opens with a personal album of family photographs. *Camera Lucida* gives up the semiological method for a subjective, phenomenological approach, which one scholar calls the end of Barthes's "long-standing suspicion of photographic representation".

### Cited Findings
- ***Mythologies* (1957).** "The Great Family of Man" analyses the photography exhibition *The Family of Man* as a political myth of a universal human condition that erases history and difference — [*Mythologies* (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythologies_(book)); [text of "The Great Family of Man" (PDF)](https://s3.amazonaws.com/arena-attachments/400998/faca1a6b1d82ab7526878fcf3a8bee7d.pdf). "Photography and Electoral Appeal" argues that candidates' portraits create a personal bond with voters and convey a way of life and an ideology rather than a programme — [*Mythologies* PDF](https://soundenvironments.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/roland-barthes-mythologies.pdf). "Shock-Photos", on an exhibition of shocking images, appears in the 1979 English selection *The Eiffel Tower and Other Mythologies* — [Dant & Gilloch, "Pictures of the past: Benjamin and Barthes on photography and history", *European Journal of Cultural Studies* (2002)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1364942002005001153).
- **Semiotic essays of the 1960s–70s.** "Le message photographique", "Rhétorique de l'image" and "Le troisième sens" are described as semiotic articles that try "to reduce the image by assimilating it to the language". The same source contrasts *Camera Lucida*'s studium (recognisable semantic units that go back to a code) with the punctum, which it connects to the "third meaning" of film stills — [*Roland Barthes*, article on popups.uliege.be](https://popups.uliege.be/0774-7136/index.php?id=1112). "Rhetoric of the Image" dates from 1964 — [KIT German studies paper (Baden/Schrey)](https://www.geistsoz.kit.edu/germanistik/downloads/BadenSchrey.pdf). In it Barthes analyses a Panzani pasta advertisement into three messages: linguistic, coded iconic and non-coded iconic — [popups.uliege.be](https://popups.uliege.be/0774-7136/index.php?id=1112).
- **English collections.** *Image-Music-Text* contains "Rhetoric of the Image", "The Third Meaning: Research notes on some Eisenstein stills" and "Diderot, Brecht, Eisenstein" — [Goodreads, *Image Music Text*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/2048915.Image_Music_Text); [PDF of *Image-Music-Text* (University of Michigan course site)](https://courses.lsa.umich.edu/jptw/wp-content/uploads/sites/23/2017/08/Barthes-ImageMusicText.pdf).
- ***Roland Barthes by Roland Barthes* (1975).** It opens with a short album of family snapshots, a "photobiographical" practice that scholars connect to *Camera Lucida* — [Springer chapter, "Roland Barthes's Ghosts: Photobiographical Influence and Legacies"](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9781137309143_11); [Dant & Gilloch (2002)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1364942002005001153).
- **The shift, in scholars' words.** "In the final stages of his career, Roland Barthes abandoned his long-standing suspicion of photographic representation to write *Camera Lucida*, at once an elegy to his dead mother and a treatise on photography" — [Project MUSE book page, apparently Geoffrey Batchen (ed.), *Photography Degree Zero: Reflections on Roland Barthes's Camera Lucida* (MIT Press, 2009)](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/3596). Wording VERIFIED in a search snippet. I am inferring that this is the Batchen volume from the MUSE ID and search context. *Camera Lucida* is Barthes's "first and only book devoted to photography" — [Wikipedia, "Camera Lucida (book)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_Lucida_(book)).
- **The book retracts itself.** Batchen's essay on the book, published in *Cabinet* 36, follows the book's construction after the *Mourning Diary* appeared — [Batchen, *Cabinet* (2009–10)](https://cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php). The "palinode" (recantation) at the end of Part One, section 24, is covered in `book_text_and_concepts.md`.

### Inferences
- The two halves of *Camera Lucida* stage the break. Part One still tries a "casual phenomenology" (section 8, as listed by the text-and-concepts researcher) and sorts photographs into studium and punctum, a binary that still owes something to the 1960s method. Part Two (the mother) gives up generality in favour of one unreproduced photograph. "The Third Meaning" (1970), with its meaning that resists language and lives in details, is the clearest bridge between the semiologist and the author of the punctum.
- *Roland Barthes by Roland Barthes* already juxtaposes photographs of his mother with an autobiographical text. Camera Lucida inverts that arrangement: the key image of the mother is described but withheld.

## 4. Intellectual sources: Sartre's *L'Imaginaire*, phenomenology, Lacan (tuché), Proust, Zen/satori

### Takeaway
*Camera Lucida* names its sources openly. It is dedicated to Sartre's *L'Imaginaire* (1940), a phenomenology of the image. It calls its own method a "casual" phenomenology with Husserlian vocabulary, which scholars such as Andrew Fisher criticise as reducing phenomenology to its eidetic strand. Early on, the photograph's absolute particularity is aligned with "what Lacan calls the Tuché, the Occasion, the Encounter, the Real". Buddhist terms (*sunya*, *tathata*, credited to Alan Watts) and the Zen *satori* (a section title in Part One) carry the idea of pure "thatness". Proust shapes the whole project: the mourning-to-novel plan, the "Longtemps" lecture, and the search through photographs for the "truth" of a beloved face. Scholarship on the Lacan, Buddhist and Proust strands exists but I could reach it only in summary.

### Cited Findings
- **Sartre.** The book is dedicated to Sartre's *L'Imaginaire*. The French dedication reads « En hommage à *L'Imaginaire* de Sartre » — [`book_text_and_concepts.md`, citing French Wikipedia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Chambre_claire). Sartre's *L'Imaginaire* (1940) applied Husserl's ideas to images, photographs included — [Philosophy Now, "The Ontology of Photography" (issue 95)](https://philosophynow.org/issues/95/The_Ontology_of_Photography_From_Analogue_To_Digital). One reading treats the book as a response to Sartre's account of intentionality, in which consciousness constitutes the world through its intentions toward it — [search summary of secondary blog sources, e.g. Grant Faulkner](https://grantfaulkner.com/2014/08/roland-barthes-camera-lucida-absence-as-presence/). This is a secondary, non-scholarly source. The Centre Pompidou holds a recorded talk, "Barthes et Sartre : comme un roman" — [Centre Pompidou](https://www.centrepompidou.fr/fr/ressources/media/DTq0O0T) (found by the text-and-concepts researcher).
- **Phenomenology.** Andrew Fisher argues that the book's phenomenology is eidetic, aimed at the essence of photography. In his view Barthes reduces phenomenology to Husserl's eidetic version and ignores Heidegger's existential strand — [Andrew Fisher, "Beyond Barthes: Rethinking the phenomenology of photography", *Radical Philosophy* 148 (2008)](https://www.radicalphilosophy.com/article/beyond-barthes). One essay characterises the book's method as "a vague, casual, even cynical phenomenology", guided by Freud's Eros and Thanatos rather than Husserl's careful distinctions — [Artdoc, "Roland Barthes: The eidos of photography"](https://www.artdoc.photo/articles/the-eidos-of-photography). This phrase is probably Barthes's own (section 8), but the search showed it only in Artdoc's text, so the attribution to Barthes is UNVERIFIED. Barthes "explicitly refers to the phenomenological method (and Edmund Husserl) as inspirational" — [search summary of Phenomenology Online / Artdoc](https://phenomenologyonline.com/scholars/barthes-roland/). The book "moves from semiotics toward phenomenology" — [Ione Manzali, VJIC (informal essay)](https://vjic.org/vjic2/?page_id=5404).
- **Lacan (tuché).** In the opening pages (Howard trans., p. 4; section 2) Barthes describes the photograph as "the This", the absolute particular and contingent, which he calls "what Lacan calls the Tuché, the Occasion, the Encounter, the Real" (VERIFIED wording) — [Lori Wike, "Photographs and Signatures: Absence, Presence, and Temporality in Barthes and Derrida", *In Visible Culture* 3](https://www.rochester.edu/in_visible_culture/issue3/wike.htm). Wike reads the passage as invoking the Lacanian Real to describe the photograph's contingency. The *Encyclopedia of Lacanian Psychoanalysis* (No Subject) uses *Camera Lucida* as a case study for the Real — [No Subject, "Real"](https://nosubject.com/Jacques_Lacan:Real). The passage is also cited in [*Trames* 21:1 (2017), "Reading 'studium' and 'punctum'…"](https://www.kirj.ee/public/trames_pdf/2017/issue_1/Trames-2017-1-33-50.pdf). The section number 2 comes from my recollection plus the p. 4 location. The text-and-concepts researcher lists section 2 as "photography unclassifiable", which is consistent.
- **Zen / Buddhism.** In the same early passage (Howard p. 5) Barthes moves from *sunya* ("the void") to *tathata*, which he credits to Alan Watts. He glosses Sanskrit *tat* as the gesture of pointing ("that, there it is") — [search summary of reproductions of the text (vdoc.pub; ju81102 blog)](https://ju81102.wordpress.com/2018/02/19/roland-barthes-camera-lucida-1980/). Exact wording UNVERIFIED (paraphrased in results). The search could not confirm which Watts text Barthes used. One scholarly essay cites Watts's *The Way of Zen* in its notes — same search. The punctum has been read as "a satori which binds the viewer to the eidos of the photograph" — [Artdoc](https://www.artdoc.photo/articles/the-eidos-of-photography). The *Third Text* article "Overcome by Photography: *Camera Lucida* in an International Frame" (vol. 24, no. 3) notes that a discussion of how Barthes's psychoanalysis "squares with his Buddhist leanings would take us beyond the scope of the present essay". The scholarship acknowledges the tension but has not worked it out — [*Third Text*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09528821003799486). Section 21 of Part One is titled "satori" in section lists — [Course Hero section summaries, as compiled in `book_text_and_concepts.md`](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/summaries/).
- **Proust.** Barthes's 19 October 1978 lecture took its title from the first sentence of *À la recherche du temps perdu* ("Longtemps, je me suis couché de bonne heure"). It was repeated in New York as "Proust et moi". In it he announced the wish to write a novel and tied it to his mother's death — [Knight, *Revue Roland Barthes* (2018)](https://revue.roland-barthes.org/2018/diana-knight/a-quoi-donc-la-vita-nova-de-barthes-peut-elle-nous-convertir/); [Rushworth (UCL)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10162683/2/Rushworth_Barthes%20Return%20to%20Michelet%20for%20RS.pdf). Rushworth's article "Mourning, Intermittence, Proust" treats Barthes's mourning through Proust's notion of the "intermittences" of the heart — [UCL Discovery](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10047894/1/Rushworth_Mourning_Intermittence_Proust_AAM.pdf) (title only; content not read). In *Camera Lucida* the search for the photograph is a search for "the truth of the face" he had loved — [`book_text_and_concepts.md`](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/part-2-chapters-27-28-summary/) (exact Howard wording UNVERIFIED).
- **Other contexts scholars connect.** Benjamin is the main comparative figure in the history-of-photography literature (Dant & Gilloch 2002) — [EJCS](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1364942002005001153). The *Preparation of the Novel* course also draws on Michelet and on Dante's *Vita Nuova* for the idea of a "new life" — [Rushworth, "Barthes's Return to Michelet and the Question of New Life"](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10162683/2/Rushworth_Barthes%20Return%20to%20Michelet%20for%20RS.pdf); [Martin, *Poétique*](https://shs.cairn.info/revue-poetique-2008-4-page-495?lang=fr).

### Inferences
- The sources split between Part One and Part Two. Sartre, Husserl and Lacan supply the conceptual frame of Part One: the photograph as an "image" of an absent object, the eidetic search for the essence of photography, and the encounter with the Real. Proust and the mourning (the *Vita Nova*) drive Part Two. Zen thatness and *satori* run through both, as a name for the photograph's resistance to language, a theme Barthes had developed earlier around haiku in *Empire of Signs* (1970). That book was not searched, so the last point is an inference.
- The dedication to Sartre can be read as a pointed gesture in 1980: a structuralist-era critic paying homage to the phenomenologist whom structuralism had displaced. The sources found do not discuss this, so it is an inference only.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the exact Howard wording or section numbers for: the *tathata* passage (likely section 2); "a vague, casual, even cynical phenomenology" (likely section 8); the satori passage (section 21); "the truth of the face I had loved" (likely section 28); or any explicit mention of Proust in *Camera Lucida*. From recollection, Part Two mentions Proust in connection with the involuntary return of the mother's image and the "intermittences" of the heart. UNVERIFIED.
- I did not confirm through the search which seminar of Lacan's the *tuché* comes from. It is generally attributed to Seminar XI (1964, *The Four Fundamental Concepts of Psychoanalysis*), in the pair *tuché/automaton*. This is background knowledge, UNVERIFIED here.

## 5. The main biographies (Calvet, Samoyault, Gil, Stafford) and what they add about how the book was written

### Takeaway
There are four main biographies. Louis-Jean Calvet's (French 1990; English 1994, trans. Sarah Wykes) was much criticised and made the family more guarded. Marie Gil's *Roland Barthes: Au lieu de la vie* (Flammarion, 2012) reads the life "as a text". Tiphaine Samoyault's *Roland Barthes* (Seuil, 2015; English *Barthes: A Biography*, Polity, 2017, trans. Andrew Brown) is the most comprehensive, based on unpublished letters, journals and photographs. Andy Stafford's *Roland Barthes* (Reaktion "Critical Lives", 2015) is a short English life. In the summaries I could reach, the biographies' specific contributions on *how Camera Lucida was written* were thin. The most concrete new evidence about its composition comes from the posthumous *Mourning Diary* and the lecture editions, not from the biographies.

### Cited Findings
- **Calvet.** *Roland Barthes: A Biography*, trans. Sarah Wykes (1994). Sources disagree on the publisher: Cambridge: Polity Press in one citation, Bloomington: Indiana University Press in another, which calls it a "popularised" biography — [search summary of academic citations](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/765179); [Greek Wikipedia, "Ρολάν Μπαρτ"](https://el.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CE%A1%CE%BF%CE%BB%CE%AC%CE%BD_%CE%9C%CF%80%CE%B1%CF%81%CF%84). The two may be co-publishers (UK and US), but that is not confirmed. ### Inferences
- For the "how it was written" story, the report should rely on: (1) the closing note of the French edition (dates 15 April to 3 June 1979, per Batchen/Universalis); (2) the *Mourning Diary* entries of June 1978 on the photograph sorting; (3) *La Préparation du roman* and the "Longtemps" lecture for the "Vita Nova" frame.
