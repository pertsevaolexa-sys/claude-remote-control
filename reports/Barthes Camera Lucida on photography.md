# One Unseen Photograph Rewrote Photography Theory

Roland Barthes's *Camera Lucida* (*La Chambre claire: Note sur la photographie*) is a short essay in **48 numbered sections, split into two parts of 24**. Barthes wrote it in about seven weeks, **between 15 April and 3 June 1979**, on a commission from *Cahiers du cinéma*. Printing was finished on **25 January 1980**. A month later, on 25 February, a laundry van hit him, and he died on **26 March 1980**, so this was the last book he saw published. The argument turns on a failure that Barthes stages on purpose. Part One works out the now-famous pair **studium** (the photograph's general, cultural interest) and **punctum** (the chance detail that "pierces" a particular viewer) from photographs reproduced in the book, and then withdraws the method. Part Two starts again from one photograph of his dead mother, Henriette, aged five in 1898. He describes it and refuses to print it. From that image he draws photography's "noeme", **"that-has-been"**, and a second punctum, which is time itself: every photograph says that its subject existed and will die. The title is part of the argument. Barthes ties photography to the *camera lucida*, a prism drawing aid, and not to the camera obscura's "dark passage", because for him a photograph is flat evidence and has no hidden depth. Critics first answered the book with mourning tributes (Derrida, Sontag). Then came arguments over the punctum (Fried and Elkins, 2005) and the finding that Barthes misdescribed photographs (Olin). Geoffrey Batchen's 2009 collection of essays on the book is billed as being about "perhaps the most influential book ever published on photography". Most recently the book has become the standard opponent in debates about digital and AI images. Richard Howard's 1981 translation is still the only English version. It is in print from Hill and Wang (with a 2010 foreword by Geoff Dyer) and from Vintage Classics, and since 2025 there is also an audiobook. It reads best as what it partly is, an essay-novel about mourning, checked against Barthes's own *Mourning Diary*.

## Forty-nine days of writing, eight weeks left to live

### A bereavement, a lecture course and a commission converge

The book starts with a death. **Henriette Barthes, née Binger, born 1893**, "no writer but the mother of one", died on **25 October 1977** ([Michigan Quarterly Review](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/mqr/2014/06/the-private-art-on-roland-barthess-mourning-diary/); [Publishers Weekly](https://publishersweekly.com/9780809062331)). Barthes's father, the naval officer Louis Barthes, died in the First World War, and Henriette raised her son in Urt and Bayonne ([Wikipedia, "Roland Barthes"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roland_Barthes)). Tiphaine Samoyault's biography shows how close they were. It reproduces a visa request, made before his posting to Romania, asking that he be allowed to travel with his "wife", and the "wife" was his mother ([Philosophy in Review](https://journals.uvic.ca/index.php/pir/article/view/17086/7553)). The day after she died, **26 October 1977**, Barthes began writing on typing paper cut into quarters. These slips became the *Mourning Diary*, edited by Nathalie Léger and published in French in 2009 ([complete-review](https://complete-review.com/reviews/divlitnf/barthes.htm)). The published English subtitle dates the entries **26 October 1977 to 15 September 1979** ([Publishers Weekly](https://publishersweekly.com/9780809062331)). The mourning notes therefore run before, during and after the writing of *Camera Lucida*. The book did not end the grief. It came out of the middle of it.

Barthes's public career was at its peak in these same months. He gave his inaugural lecture in the chair of literary semiology at the Collège de France on **7 January 1977**, nine months before his mother died. It was later published as *Leçon* ([Éditions Points](https://www.editionspoints.com/ouvrage/lecon-roland-barthes/9782757850169)). His lecture courses moved from *How to Live Together* ([Columbia UP](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/how-to-live-together/9780231136167/)) to ***The Preparation of the Novel* (1978–80)**. That course was an experiment: he used teaching and writing to "simulate" the attempt to write a novel. Columbia's 2010 English edition includes **eight plans for the unwritten novel *Vita Nova*** and lecture notes on photography ([Columbia UP](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-preparation-of-the-novel/9780231136143/)). Barthes himself dated a "literary conversion" to **15 April 1978 in Casablanca** ([Jean-Pierre Martin, *Poétique*](https://shs.cairn.info/revue-poetique-2008-4-page-495?lang=fr)). On 19 October 1978 he gave the Proust lecture "Longtemps, je me suis couché de bonne heure", in which he announced that he wanted to write a novel and linked that wish to his mother's death ([Diana Knight, *Revue Roland Barthes*](https://revue.roland-barthes.org/2018/diana-knight/a-quoi-donc-la-vita-nova-de-barthes-peut-elle-nous-convertir/)). Knight has asked why Barthes presented his mourning as a conversion, "a singular and sudden event" ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/603957)).

The commission came into this situation. **Jean Narboni**, of the *Cahiers du cinéma* editorial staff, proposed the book ([*24 images*, Érudit](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/images/2016-n177-images02511/81944ac.pdf)). Barthes wrote it between 15 April and 3 June 1979, in under two months ([*24 images*, Érudit](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/images/2016-n177-images02511/81944ac.pdf)); Universalis gives the same span as "d'avril à juin 1979" ([Universalis](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/la-chambre-claire/)). Batchen counts **forty-nine days** ([Cabinet](https://cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php)). None of my sources notes that the first day of writing fell exactly one year after the dated Casablanca "conversion". That may be chance, but it fits the book's place inside the *Vita Nova* project. The French edition appeared under a joint imprint, "Cahiers du cinéma" : Gallimard : Seuil, 1980, 192 pages ([BnF catalogue](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb34632476d)). Printing was finished on 25 January 1980 ([*24 images*, Érudit](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/images/2016-n177-images02511/81944ac.pdf)), and Universalis gives February 1980 as the publication date. Page counts differ between records (192, 196, 200, 202), which reflects reprints and counting conventions, not different texts. The book is dedicated **« En hommage à *L'Imaginaire* de Sartre »** ([French Wikipedia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Chambre_claire)). French reviewers were mixed ([Érudit](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/images/2016-n177-images02511/81944ac.pdf)).

On **25 February 1980**, at about 3:30 pm, Barthes was walking back along the rue des Écoles after a lunch with François Mitterrand, then leader of the Socialist Party, when a laundry van struck him ([Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/entertainment/books/who-killed-roland-barthes-maybe-umberto-eco-has-a-clue/2017/08/22/6f644c8e-8696-11e7-a94f-3139abce39f5_story.html); [*Literary Review*](https://literaryreview.co.uk/death-of-an-author)). He was taken to the Pitié-Salpêtrière and died on **26 March 1980, aged 64** ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/barthes-roland-1915-1980)). Sources disagree on the cause: pulmonary complications according to the *Post*, a hospital-acquired infection according to [*24 images*](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/images/2016-n177-images02511/81944ac.pdf). A few sources give 25 March as the date, but 26 March is better supported. Laurent Binet's 2015 novel *La Septième Fonction du langage* imagines the accident as a murder. It is fiction, not evidence. Barthes never saw the English edition: Richard Howard's translation came out from **Hill and Wang, New York, in 1981** ([WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Camera-lucida-:-reflections-on-photography/oclc/7307145)). Records give its month as February or June ([Stedelijk library record](https://library.stedelijk.nl/Details/books/7620)), and Howard does not recall Barthes correcting it ([Batchen, *Cabinet*](https://cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php)).

### The last book overturns the semiologist's suspicion of photographs

The book matters partly because it contradicts Barthes's earlier work. In *Mythologies* (1957) he took apart the photography exhibition *The Family of Man* as a myth of a universal human condition that erases history. He read election portraits as carriers of ideology rather than political programmes ([*Mythologies*, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythologies_(book))). His essays of the 1960s, "The Photographic Message" and "Rhetoric of the Image" (1964), tried to "reduce the image by assimilating it to the language". The second divides a Panzani pasta advertisement into linguistic, coded-iconic and non-coded-iconic messages ([*Roland Barthes*, popups.uliege.be](https://popups.uliege.be/0774-7136/index.php?id=1112)). "The Third Meaning" (1970), on stills from Eisenstein's films, already looked for a meaning that escapes the code. The same scholarship treats it as the forerunner of the punctum ([popups.uliege.be](https://popups.uliege.be/0774-7136/index.php?id=1112)). *Roland Barthes by Roland Barthes* (1975) opens with an album of family snapshots, a "photobiographical" habit that *Camera Lucida* turns around ([Springer, "Roland Barthes's Ghosts"](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9781137309143_11)). There the mother's photographs were shown. Here the decisive one is withheld. *Camera Lucida* is Barthes's **"first and only book devoted to photography"** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_Lucida_(book))). The publisher's description of Jean-Michel Rabaté's collection *Writing the Image After Roland Barthes* says that Barthes "abandoned his long-standing suspicion of photographic representation to write *Camera Lucida*, at once an elegy to his dead mother and a treatise on photography" ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/3596)). Jeff Fort reads the book as a revolt against Barthes's own former critical positions ([Fort, *Mosaic* 2020](https://ucdavis.academia.edu/JFort/Papers)).

Barthes names his sources openly. The Sartre dedication points to a phenomenology of the image that applied Husserl's ideas to pictures, photographs included ([Philosophy Now](https://philosophynow.org/issues/95/The_Ontology_of_Photography_From_Analogue_To_Digital)). Andrew Fisher argues that Barthes narrows phenomenology to Husserl's "eidetic" search for essences and ignores Heidegger's existential strand ([*Radical Philosophy* 148](https://www.radicalphilosophy.com/article/beyond-barthes)). Early on, Barthes calls the photograph's absolute particularity "what Lacan calls the Tuché, the Occasion, the Encounter, the Real" ([Lori Wike, *In Visible Culture*](https://www.rochester.edu/in_visible_culture/issue3/wike.htm)). One Part One section is titled "Satori", and a *Third Text* essay notes that scholars have not reconciled Barthes's psychoanalysis with his "Buddhist leanings" ([*Third Text*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09528821003799486)). Proust shapes the second half. Barthes describes a photograph he does not reproduce, much as Proust does, and compares the experience to involuntary memory ([*STTCL*](https://ksu.janeway.systems/sttcl/article/11900/galley/11784/download)). Benjamin is present more quietly. Many of the plates came from sources close at hand, above all the November 1977 photography issue of *Le Nouvel Observateur*, which carried a French translation of Benjamin's "Little History of Photography" ([Batchen, *Cabinet*](https://www.cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php)). My reading of this background is that *Camera Lucida* belongs to the *Preparation of the Novel* project as much as to photography theory. It is the one finished piece of the "new life" of writing that Barthes was lecturing about.

## Forty-eight sections build a theory, retract it, and start again

### Part One builds a vocabulary for the viewer (§§1–24)

The book opens with surprise. Barthes comes across an **1852 photograph of Jérôme, Napoleon's youngest brother**, and is struck that he is looking at eyes that looked at the Emperor. That moment prompts the question of what photography is "in itself" ([SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/camera-lucida/summary/); [MIT photo course blog](https://mitphoto2016.wordpress.com/2016/04/29/a-not-so-short-summary-of-camera-lucida/)). The French table of contents gives each section a title. The first three are "Spécialité de la Photo", "La Photo inclassable" and "L'émotion comme départ" ([Google Books](https://books.google.de/books/about/La_chambre_claire.html?id=l4wHAQAAIAAJ&redir_esc=y)). Barthes argues that photography resists the usual classifications and decides to take his own feeling as his guide. In **§4** he names the three practices of photography, "to do, to undergo, and to look", and the three positions that go with them. The **Operator** is the photographer, the **Spectator** is the viewer, and the **Spectrum** is the person or thing photographed. He chooses "Spectrum" because the word suggests both spectacle and *spectre*, the return of the dead. He sets the Operator aside because he is not a photographer ([UChicago CSMT annotation](http://csmt.uchicago.edu/annotations/barthescamera2.htm)). Writing about the experience of being photographed, he arrives at the line "Death is the eidos of that Photograph" ([Garage](https://garagemca.org/en/programs/publishing/roland-barthes-roland-barthes-camera-lucida-reflections-on-photography)). From the first pages, then, death is the essence (*eidos*) behind the image.

The famous terms appear in **§§9–10**. A 1979 photograph of Nicaragua by **Koen Wessing** catches Barthes through an incongruous pair, soldiers in the street and nuns passing behind them. That "duality" yields the two terms ([Course Hero](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/summaries/)). The **studium** is "a kind of education (knowledge and civility, 'politeness')" that lets a viewer recognise what the photographer intended ([nouspique](https://nouspique.com/barthes-studium-and-punctum/)). The **punctum** is the element that "rises from the scene, shoots out of it like an arrow, and pierces me" ([MIT photo course blog](https://mitphoto2016.wordpress.com/2016/04/29/a-not-so-short-summary-of-camera-lucida/)). The next sections go through the studium's forms. Richard Avedon's portrait of William Casby, "born a slave", is a "mask" that lays bare the essence of slavery ([SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/camera-lucida/summary/)). Charles Clifford's 1854 Alhambra is a landscape Barthes wants to live in ([studylib](https://studylib.net/doc/5225214/roland-barthes-camera-lucida)). **§17** defines the **"unary" photograph**, an image whose studium no punctum disturbs, which makes it banal ([MIT photo course blog](https://mitphoto2016.wordpress.com/2016/04/29/a-not-so-short-summary-of-camera-lucida/)).

Sections 18 to 23 study the punctum as a detail. Their French titles are "Co-présence du Studium et du Punctum", "Punctum : trait partiel", "Trait involontaire", "Satori", "Après coup et silence" and "Champ aveugle" ([livres-cinema.info](https://www.livres-cinema.info/livre/11923/chambre-claire)). The examples became textbook cases. In James Van Der Zee's 1926 family portrait the punctum is a woman's strapped pumps. In a William Klein photograph it is a child's bad teeth. In André Kertész's photograph of a blind violinist it is the dirt road, which brings back villages Barthes had passed through in Hungary and Romania ([SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/camera-lucida/summary/); [Kirkus](https://kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/roland-barthes/camera-lucida/print)). In §20 Barthes insists that the punctum must not be intended by the photographer, a claim Michael Fried would later build an argument on ([PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/FRIBP)). In §22 Barthes corrects himself. On reflection, the Van Der Zee punctum is not the shoes but the woman's necklace, because the image had kept "working" in him after he stopped looking ([Media-studies.com](https://media-studies.com/studium-and-punctum/)). The text shows its own method shifting as it goes.

Then, at **§24 ("Palinodie")**, it gives the method up. Barthes says he must make his *palinodie*, which Howard renders with a doubled phrase, "my recantation, my palinode" ([Batchen, caa.reviews](http://www.caareviews.org/reviews/1482)). (The exact French sentence could not be checked against the text.) A palinode is a poet's formal retraction of an earlier poem. Barthes's reason is that his pleasure was an imperfect guide. It had shown him how his desire worked, but not what makes a photograph different from every other image ([caa.reviews](http://www.caareviews.org/reviews/1482)). Part One generalised from many photographs toward a vocabulary any viewer could use. Part Two refuses generality.

### Part Two starts again from one photograph (§§25–48)

Part Two opens "one November evening, shortly after my mother's death", with Barthes going through her photographs ([Elsa Dorfman review](http://archive.elsadorfman.com/barthes.htm)). In Andrew King's Cape-paginated study guide this falls on page 63 ([King, Annotations 2](https://blogs.gre.ac.uk/andrewking/2013/03/07/roland-barthes-camera-lucida-a-study-guide-2/)). Most of the pictures give him her likeness but not her being. He recognises her only in fragments, until **§28, "La Photographie du Jardin d'Hiver"**, where he finds her in a picture taken when "my mother was five at the time (1898)" ([livres-cinema.info](https://www.livres-cinema.info/livre/11923/chambre-claire); [Course Hero, Part 2 ch. 27–28](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/part-2-chapters-27-28-summary/)). In **§30, "Ariadne"**, the photograph becomes the thread through the labyrinth, the single image from which he will now think all of Photography ([MIT photo course blog](https://mitphoto2016.wordpress.com/2016/11/15/barthes-camera-lucida/)). In the same section he refuses to print it. "I cannot reproduce the Winter Garden Photograph. It exists only for me" ([Perloff, EPC/UPenn](https://writing.upenn.edu/epc/authors/perloff/boltanski.html)). For anyone else it would be "nothing but an indifferent picture" ([UT Austin DWRL](https://viz.dwrl.utexas.edu/old/content/roland-barthes-photography.html)).

From this one image comes the book's central claim. In **§32** Barthes writes: «Le nom du noème de la Photographie sera donc : "Ça-a-été"» ([Littérature et Photographie](https://littetphotlma0401v.over-blog.com/2017/03/roland-barthes-la-chambre-claire-1980-extraits.html)). Howard's version is "That-has-been," or again: the Intractable ([Goodreads quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/799260-la-chambre-claire-note-sur-la-photographie)). A noeme, in Husserl's vocabulary, is the object as consciousness intends it. Barthes's point is that a photograph certifies that its referent really stood in front of the lens, however briefly ([académie de Montpellier fiche](https://pedagogie.ac-montpellier.fr/sites/default/files/ressources/FL%20Roland%20Barthes%20La%20chambre%20claire.pdf)). The following sections argue this physically. "La pose" (§33) grounds photography in the fact that the subject was there ([livres-cinema.info](https://www.livres-cinema.info/livre/11923/chambre-claire)). Then: «La photo est littéralement une émanation du référent. D'un corps réel, qui était là, sont parties des radiations qui viennent me toucher» ([bonnespioches](http://bonnespioches.unblog.fr/2018/07/20/la-chambre-claire-roland-barthes/)). Light connects the dead body to the living viewer.

**§39** turns the noeme back into the punctum. Under Alexander Gardner's 1865 portrait of **Lewis Payne**, photographed in April 1865 while held prisoner aboard a navy monitor (Barthes says "in his cell") before he was hanged that July ([Ghosts of DC](https://ghostsofdc.org/2014/01/16/colorized-photo-lewis-powell-lincoln-conspirator/)), Barthes writes: "He is dead and he is going to die." "The photograph is handsome, as is the boy: that is the studium. But the punctum is: he is going to die" ([Art Blart](https://artblart.com/tag/hot-dead-guys-lewis-powell/); [PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2013/10/25/assassin-camera/)). This second punctum, "no longer of form but of intensity, is Time" (["Loss and Found", Taylor & Francis, 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17514517.2024.2353472)). Sections 40 to 43 deal with private and public images, the uselessness of enlarging a photograph to get more out of it, likeness, and family lineage ([Course Hero](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/summaries/)). The section that names the book (§44) comes next, and then **§45, "The Air"**, on the quality of a face that a photograph sometimes catches ([Course Hero](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/summaries/)). The book ends with **§47, "Madness, Pity"**, and **§48, "The Photograph Tamed"**. Photography is "a bizarre medium, a new form of hallucination: false on the level of perception, true on the level of time" ([MIT photo course blog](https://mitphoto2016.wordpress.com/2018/04/12/camera-lucida-by-roland-barthes/)), "a mad image, chafed by reality" ([caa.reviews](http://www.caareviews.org/reviews/1482)). "Society is concerned to tame the Photograph, to temper the madness which keeps threatening to explode in the face of whoever looks at it" ([Course Hero](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/summaries/)). The last page leaves the reader to choose between facing the photograph's "intractable reality" and taming it into harmless illusion ([Batchen, *Cabinet*](https://www.cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php)).

The thing to notice is that there are **two puncta**. In Part One the punctum is a matter of *form*: a necklace or a pair of shoes that only one viewer sees. In Part Two it becomes a matter of *intensity*: time and death, which are in every photograph for every viewer. Many popular summaries keep only the first, the detail that catches your eye, and so lose the book's main claim. The second punctum is the noeme of §32 felt as a wound. It is how Barthes gets from his private grief back to a universal statement about photography after the palinode had ruled out generalising. One caution about sources: section titles beyond those cited above come from study guides that use English titles of their own. Cite the book by section number, which every edition shares.

## Twenty-five plates surround one picture nobody sees

### What Barthes reproduces, and what he does with it

The French edition has **25 images chosen by Barthes**, old and new ([French Wikipedia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Chambre_claire)). Batchen counts "only twenty-four" illustrated photographs, reproduced in black and white and mostly taken from material close at hand ([Cabinet](https://www.cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php)). The two counts agree once you add the frontispiece. That is **Daniel Boudinet's *Polaroïd* (1979)**, a blue-green image of a curtain and the book's only colour plate. Barthes never mentions it in the text. He first saw it at a Boudinet exhibition opening in April 1979, while he was writing the book ([Cabinet](https://www.cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php)). The first US edition also prints it in colour, and a bookseller's listing describes the 1982 Cape edition the same way ([ZVAB, Cape 1982 listing](https://www.zvab.com/buch-suchen/isbn/9780224029292/)). Not every edition keeps it: Batchen reports that it was dropped from a 1989 Spanish edition and from the German, Czech, British and one Farsi version, and that several others print it only in black and white ([Cabinet](https://www.cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php)). French Wikipedia's "Corpus" gives the full list of photographers with the French page numbers, and it adds up to 25 ([French Wikipedia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Chambre_claire)).

### The Winter Garden Photograph: grief, staging and the debate over its existence

The Winter Garden Photograph is the **absent centre** of the book. It is the snapshot of Henriette aged five in 1898, and it is the only image Barthes discusses at length without reproducing it ([Photoworks, "The Great Unknown"](https://photoworks.org.uk/great-unknown/)). French reviewers note that the founding photograph of the book is the one that is missing ([Jacques Ould Aoudia](https://jacques-ould-aoudia.net/la-chambre-claire-roland-barthes-note-de-lecture/)). His own theory explains why he withholds it. The punctum belongs to one viewer and cannot be passed on. Printed, the photograph would become plain studium for the reader, an "indifferent picture". Withholding it also prepares the reversal that follows: an image that wounds only one viewer becomes, through Time, the source of a punctum that every photograph carries.

The *Mourning Diary* shows that the book also stages the scene. Commentators date the diary's first reference to looking at "her photographs" to **11 June 1978**, "not November of the previous year as he claims in *Camera Lucida*". They also note that Barthes was not alone but with his half-brother Michel Salzedo. An entry they date 13 June 1978 describes a photograph of his mother as a little girl beside her brother Philippe Binger, labelled **"the Winter Garden of Chennevières, 1898"** ([Photoworks](https://photoworks.org.uk/great-unknown/); [*Between*](https://ojs.unica.it/index.php/between/article/download/1579/1585/)). So the book moves the discovery about seven months earlier, to "one November evening" just after the death, and makes Barthes alone. That is a novelist's compression, which fits the *Vita Nova* project he was lecturing on at the time.

This matters for the longest-running dispute about the book. Margaret Olin argued that the photograph as Barthes describes it may never have existed. On her reading it is an imagined alteration of another family picture, with a grandfather removed and a winter garden added. She also showed that Barthes misdescribes the Van Der Zee portrait: he writes of a braided gold necklace, but the woman is wearing pearls ([IVC review of Olin, *Touching Photographs*](https://ivc.lib.rochester.edu/?p=4802)). From this she argues the book should be read as a novel ([Photoworks](https://photoworks.org.uk/great-unknown/)). My reading of the evidence is that the diary makes it very likely that a real 1898 photograph of Henriette as a child, labelled as taken in a winter garden, existed and was the image Barthes meant. Commentators take the entry as the end of the non-existence speculation. What the diary undermines is not the photograph but the book's account of how he found it. That moves the question from fact to genre. Olin's strong claim (no such photograph) looks weaker, and her broader point (Barthes shapes what he sees) looks stronger. No source I found says whether the print survives in an archive, so that question remains open.

The refusal has itself become productive. Critics call it "a brilliant rhetorical manoeuvre" that invites every reader to put their own dead into the empty space. They also note its "doppelgänger" in the undiscussed Boudinet Polaroid at the front of the book ([Photoworks](https://photoworks.org.uk/great-unknown/)). Marjorie Perloff set it beside Christian Boltanski's archives of anonymous photographs of the dead ([Perloff](https://writing.upenn.edu/epc/authors/perloff/boltanski.html)). For the book's 40th anniversary, Odette England's ***Keeper of the Hearth: Picturing Roland Barthes' Unseen Photograph* (2020)** collected more than 200 photographers, writers, critics and historians imagining the image. It was published as a 320-page book and shown at the Houston Center for Photography from September 2020 to January 2021 ([LensCulture](https://www.lensculture.com/articles/odette-england-keeper-of-the-hearth); [CPH Mag](https://cphmag.com/barthes-unseen/); [F-Stop Magazine](https://www.fstopmagazine.com/blog/2020/keeper-of-the-hearth-picturing-roland-barthes-unseen-photograph-houston-center-for-photography/)).

## A prism, not a darkroom, gives the book its name

The title comes from an optical instrument that predates photography. The **camera lucida**, patented by **William Hyde Wollaston in 1806**, is a prism usually mounted on an arm above a drawing board. Through two internal reflections it produces a virtual image of the scene that seems to lie on the paper, so the draughtsman can trace it ([Wikipedia, "Camera lucida"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_lucida)). There is no box and no darkness. It is tied to photography's invention by a failure. In October 1833, sketching with a camera lucida on the shore of Lake Como, William Henry Fox Talbot was frustrated by his drawings, and that frustration pushed him toward fixing images chemically ([Photographic Historical Society of Canada](https://phsc.ca/camera/lucy/)).

Barthes turns the device into an argument. "It is camera Lucida that we should say (such was the name of that apparatus, anterior to Photography, which permitted drawing an object through a prism, one eye on the model, the other on the paper)" ([Boise Photo Co.](https://www.boisephoto.co/blog/best-quotes-from-camera-lucida-by-roland-barthes)). He introduces it through its opposite, the camera obscura. That darkened box, which projects an image onto a surface, is photography's technical ancestor, and Barthes says it is a mistake to associate photography with it ([Andrew King, Greenwich study guide](https://blogs.gre.ac.uk/andrewking/?p=311)). The French title *La Chambre claire* ("the bright room") translates *camera lucida* literally and inverts *chambre noire*, the French term for both darkroom and camera obscura. Howard's English title restores the Latin, and the German *Die helle Kammer* and the Japanese 明るい部屋 ("bright room") keep the pun. Barthes's "one eye on the model, the other on the paper" is a loose account of how the prism works, since in Wollaston's design the scene appears superimposed on the paper. What he wants from the device, though, is clear.

That point is about **essence, not technology**. The camera obscura suggests depth, an inside, something hidden that interpretation could open up. The camera lucida suggests transparency, with the scene and the image present together and nothing behind them. Barthes's photograph cannot be "developed" or explored. It shows, with the plain certainty of "that-has-been", and enlarging it yields nothing more ([Course Hero](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Camera-Lucida/summaries/)). There is a second resonance, which is my interpretation rather than anything in the sources. Batchen notes that in a camera lucida the image is visible **only to the draughtsman** and reaches anyone else only as a tracing ([Cabinet](https://cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php)). That is the situation of the Winter Garden Photograph, which "exists only for me" and reaches the reader only as Barthes's verbal tracing. The first French cover made the title literal. It showed an engraving of a camera lucida in use, taken from Beaumont Newhall's *History of Photography*, and a later cover replaced it with a sketch of a small camera on a tripod ([Batchen, *Cabinet*](https://www.cabinetmagazine.org/issues/36/batchen.php)).

## Reception moved from elegy to battleground to test case for AI images

### Mourners and rereaders, 1980–2005

Barthes died weeks after publication, so the first serious responses were tributes. Jacques Derrida's "Les morts de Roland Barthes" (*Poétique* 47, 1981) reads the book as haunted by ghosts that "permeate its fabric and logic and form the category of 'punctum'" ([nbpublish](https://en.nbpublish.com/library_read_article.php?id=71369)). For Derrida the book itself becomes a wound and an occasion for mourning ([UT Austin DWRL](https://viz.dwrl.utexas.edu/old/content/roland-barthes-photography.html)). The essay is collected in *The Work of Mourning* (Chicago, 2001) ([NYU Medical Humanities](https://medhum.med.nyu.edu/view/12930)). Susan Sontag's brief eulogy "Remembering Barthes" appeared in *Under the Sign of Saturn* in 1980 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Under_the_Sign_of_Saturn)). The *New York Review of Books* reviewed the English translation in December 1981 ([NYRB](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/12/17/that-old-black-and-white-magic/)). Then came the rereadings. Victor Burgin's "Re-reading *Camera Lucida*" appeared in *The End of Art Theory* (1986), and he returned to it in 2018 in *The Camera: Essence and Apparatus* ([et-al.ophen](https://et-al.ophen.org/pub-207170); [UW–Madison catalogue](https://search.library.wisc.edu/catalog/9910080664002121)). Diana Knight's "Roland Barthes, or The Woman Without a Shadow" appeared in Rabaté's *Writing the Image After Roland Barthes* ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/3596)). Jay Prosser's *Light in the Dark Room* (2005) opens with "Roland Barthes's Loss" and reads the punctum through the Lacanian Real ([SMU catalogue](https://smu.vufind.net/smu/Record/a20054331/Details?lng=es)).

### The punctum quarrel and the field-defining collection

In 2005 *Critical Inquiry* turned the book into an argument about photography as art. Michael Fried's "Barthes's Punctum" (vol. 31, no. 3) argued that the usual subjective reading misses Barthes's main thought, the §20 claim that the punctum must not be intended. Fried read the book as "antitheatrical" and fitted it into his own *Absorption and Theatricality* framework ([PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/FRIBP); [Fillip](https://fillip.ca/content/frieds-turn)). James Elkins replied in the next issue ([*Critical Inquiry* 31.4](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/epdf/10.1086/444520)). In *What Photography Is* (2011) Elkins wrote "against" the book, which he found "too full of light", in favour of photography as meaninglessness and the difficulty of seeing ([Elkins](https://jameselkins.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/05/what-photography-is.pdf)). Kathrin Yacavone points out that Elkins copies Barthes's short numbered sections even while calling the book "half-ruined theory" ([Yacavone review](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/737432)). Olin's "mistaken identification" essay brought the book to an empirical test ([IVC](https://ivc.lib.rochester.edu/?p=4802)).

**Geoffrey Batchen's *Photography Degree Zero: Reflections on Roland Barthes's Camera Lucida* (MIT Press, 2009)** collected essays by Burgin, Jane Gallop, Margaret Iversen, Olin, Prosser, Eduardo Cadava and Paola Cortés-Rocca, Fried, Elkins, Rosalind Krauss, Gordon Hughes, Carol Mavor and Shawn Michelle Smith ([AUT library TOC](https://librarysearch.aut.ac.nz/vufind/Record/1203218/TOC)). The approaches run "from psychoanalytical to Buddhist" and include two readings through race. The publisher's description calls *Camera Lucida* **"perhaps the most influential book ever published on photography"** ([MIT Press Bookstore](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780262516662)). Later work defines itself against the book while conceding its importance. Kaja Silverman's *The Miracle of Analogy* rejects the indexical model associated with Barthes and proposes "analogy" in its place ([nonsite.org](https://nonsite.org/the-miracle-of-analogy)). *Mosaic* 53.4 (December 2020) marked the 40th anniversary with eight essays, an interview with Ian Wallace and a curated section of André Zoungrana's photographs ([*Mosaic*](https://mosaic.umanitoba.ca/common/issue/get/53/4)). In 2025 a *Social Semiotics* article asked what the book still offers after forty years ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10350330.2025.2453991)), and Snježana Šimić argued that it is more phenomenological than Barthes admitted ([Šimić](https://ehess.academia.edu/Snje%C5%BEana%C5%A0imi%C4%87)).

### "That-has-been" under digital and generative pressure

"That-has-been" became the standard statement of photography's indexicality, its causal tie to a real referent, so every change in imaging technology has tested it. William J. Mitchell's *The Reconfigured Eye* (1992) gave an early full account of digital imaging eroding the photograph's assumed truthfulness ([AbeBooks listing](https://www.abebooks.com/9780262132862/Reconfigured-Eye-Visual-Truth-Post-Photographic-0262132869/plp)). Mary Ann Doane's 2007 *differences* issue "Indexicality: Trace and Sign", with Tom Gunning's "Moving Away from the Index", questioned whether the index was ever as strong a concept as theorists claimed ([Duke UP](https://dukeupress.edu/indexicality-trace-and-sign)). Joel Snyder argued that index claims confuse photons with objects ([Wasielewski, Aalto 2026](https://helsinkiphotomedia.aalto.fi/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/WASIELEWSKI_AMANDA_Light_in_the_Distance.pdf)). Generative AI has sharpened the challenge. A 2026 article in *RIFL* argues that for photorealistic AI images "that-has-been" gives way to "that-could-be", with a "certification of presence" replaced by a "certification of plausibility" ([*RIFL*](https://rifl.unical.it/index.php/rifl/article/view/944)). Joan Fontcuberta has made Barthes his named opponent twice: in the exhibition *Ça-a-été? Contra Barthes* at La Virreina, Barcelona (October 2021 to early 2022), and in ***Against Barthes: The Eye and the Index*** (MACK, April 2025), which "refutes, as well as draws on" the noeme ([La Virreina](https://ajuntament.barcelona.cat/lavirreina/en/exhibitions/ca-ete-against-barthes/542); [MACK](https://mackbooks.co.uk/products/against-barthes-the-eye-and-the-index-joan-fontcuberta)).

My view is that the noeme survives this pressure better than its critics suggest, provided it is read correctly. Read as an *ontological* claim about silver halides and light, "that-has-been" really is undercut by digital compositing and generative images. But Barthes reached it as a *phenomenological* claim about what a viewer believes when looking at a photograph, and he hedged it with the idea of photography as hallucination, "false on the level of perception, true on the level of time". On that reading the AI era does not refute the book. It shows that "that-has-been" was always a belief viewers bring to an image, and now viewers have to ask whether it applies. That helps explain why Fontcuberta argues with Barthes instead of ignoring him, and why Elkins, after all his objections, concedes that the book "remains a central text" ([Elkins](https://jameselkins.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/05/what-photography-is.pdf)). One gap: I found no scholarship that deals specifically with the smartphone snapshot, as distinct from "digital" or "AI" images, in relation to Barthes.

### Writers, artists and the syllabus

The book's literary influence is strongest in prose that mixes text and photographs. Archival work by Kevin Brazil shows that W. G. Sebald first read Barthes in the early 1990s. *The Emigrants* deliberately breaks images' indexical ties, which amounts to rejecting the book's ontology. Sebald came back to Barthes while writing *Austerlitz* and revised the draft so that the novel refers directly to *Camera Lucida* ([Brazil, *Textual Practice* 2019](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/397722/)). Teju Cole's *Blind Spot* (2017) "owes more to Barthes, whose *Camera Lucida* theorized good photography as that which wounds" ([Ploughshares](https://pshares.org/blog/the-role-of-images-in-teju-coles-blind-spot/)). Neil Badmington's *Punctum Saliens* links the book to mourning and film ([Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/35996/)). In teaching it forms a triad with Benjamin's 1931 "Small History of Photography" and Sontag's *On Photography* (1977). The Edinburgh history-of-photography course treats the three as the central twentieth-century texts ([University of Edinburgh](https://www.drps.ed.ac.uk/13-14/dpt/cxhiar11047.htm)). Martha Schwendener notes that this canon is entirely Euro-American ([*Flusser Studies*](https://www.flusserstudies.net/sites/www.flusserstudies.net/files/media/attachments/schwendener-vilem-flusser-theories-photography.pdf)). Of the three, Barthes is the one read for affect, phenomenology and mourning. Writers borrow his form (fragments, embedded photographs, a first-person grief) more readily than his ontology.

The text is short, about 119 pages in Howard's translation, and can be read in an afternoon. Reading it well takes more care. First, **read Part One as a set-up that will be withdrawn**. Studium and punctum are tools Barthes builds in order to show their limits, and the palinode at §24 is the turning point of the book. Second, **keep the plates in front of you and check his descriptions**, as Olin did. The gap between what Barthes says he sees and what is in the picture is part of the meaning. Third, **watch for the second punctum** at §39. That is where the private wound becomes a claim about every photograph. Fourth, **read it alongside the *Mourning Diary*** (especially the June 1978 entries) and *The Preparation of the Novel*. Together they show the book as a staged, novelistic work of mourning and not a neutral treatise ([Photoworks](https://photoworks.org.uk/great-unknown/); [Columbia UP](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-preparation-of-the-novel/9780231136143/)). Cite by section number, since page numbers vary between editions. Andrew King's free, section-keyed study guide is the best companion, with reading questions and Cape pagination ([King, before reading](https://blogs.gre.ac.uk/andrewking/2013/03/07/roland-barthes-camera-lucida-a-study-guide-before-reading-the-text/); [Annotations 1](https://blogs.gre.ac.uk/andrewking/2013/03/07/roland-barthes-camera-lucida-a-study-guide-1/)). The Chicago School of Media Theory annotations are also useful ([CSMT](https://csmt.uchicago.edu/annotations/barthescamera.htm)). For criticism, read Batchen's *Photography Degree Zero* ([MIT Press](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262516662/photography-degree-zero/)), then Fontcuberta's *Against Barthes* for the case against the book as it stands in 2026.

## Conclusion

The book's authority rests on something no reader can check. Its whole theory comes out of a photograph it will not show, and the diary shows that Barthes even moved the scene of finding it. That is not a defect hidden inside the theory. It is the book's method: a "note on photography" written as if it were a chapter of the novel Barthes never wrote, proving its claims by testimony rather than demonstration. This explains why *Camera Lucida* has outlasted more rigorous rivals. Critics cannot settle it from the outside. They can only test Barthes's seeing against their own, as Olin did with the necklace, Fried with intention and Fontcuberta with the index, and each test brings readers back to the text.

The noeme matters more now than it did in 1980, but in a different form. When Barthes wrote, "that-has-been" described what a photograph is. In a world of composited and generated images it has become something viewers must ask of an image: has this been? Barthes's closing choice between a "mad" photography that confronts reality and a "tamed" one that turns it into harmless illusion now works less as an elegy than as a guide to looking at images. Readers who take only the punctum as a detail that catches the eye miss the book's most lasting claim. Each photograph, by certifying that its subject existed, also shows that the subject will die, and that recognition, not any detail, is the "wound" at the book's centre.
