The Grout Bill

Mould in steamy bathrooms and kitchens: how common it is, how much cleaning chemistry a household uses to keep it down, and whether ceramic tiles still pay off over twenty years.

The short answer

The idea is partly right. Glazed tiles barely absorb water, so mould does not grow in the tile itself. It grows in the cement grout and silicone joints, and it keeps coming back while the room stays humid.

In the shower zone that upkeep adds up. Over 20 years, a wall of classic 20×25 cm tiles with cement grout costs about twice as much as grout-free wall panels: 1.9–2.1 times as much in cash, and 2.3–2.5 times if your scrubbing time counts. The result holds with German, British and American prices.

The chemicals themselves are a small part of it. A typical tiled shower gets through about 3 litres of chlorine mould spray a year, roughly €24, £18 or $17. The real money goes on regrouting, silicone renewals and hours of scrubbing.

Two changes close most of the gap: a working extractor fan, and epoxy grout or large tiles when you renovate. Kitchen splashbacks stay dry most of the day, so there tiles remain a cheap and durable choice.

How big the mould problem is

Damp and mould are common on both sides of the Atlantic. The World Health Organization estimates that 10–50% of indoor environments in Europe, North America, Australia, India and Japan are affected.[2] The surveys below measure dampness in general: leaks, condensation and rising damp. None of them counts mould on shower grout separately, so no official figure for mouldy bathrooms exists.

Why the steam zone

Every bath or shower puts about a litre of water into the air. UK housing guidance gives these rough figures:[10]

ActivityWater released into the air
A bath or shower≈1.1 L (2 pints)
Cooking and the kettle, per day≈3.4 L (6 pints)
Drying a load of washing indoors≈5.1 L (9 pints)
Two people at home for 16 hours≈1.7 L (3 pints)

That water condenses on the coldest surfaces first: outside walls, window reveals, the ceiling above the shower and the joints between tiles. Three numbers decide whether it turns into mould.

80%

relative humidity at a surface. Held above this for days, mould can grow. European and international standards use it as the design limit for walls.[11]

24–48 h

Damp materials dried within this window usually stay free of mould.[12]

15 l/s

England’s minimum bathroom fan rate. Kitchens need 30 l/s with a cooker hood, 60 without. The US asks for 50 cfm on demand or 20 cfm continuous, and 100 cfm for a range hood.[13][14]

The standard behind the 80% limit accepts brief condensation on surfaces that don’t absorb water, and names bathroom tiles as the example, provided the water can’t reach the materials behind.[11] Tiles are made for steam. The joints between them are the weak point.

Where the mould actually lives

A tiled wall is four materials, and they behave very differently in a steamy room.

MaterialTakes up water?What mould doesUpkeep
Glazed ceramic or porcelain tile Barely. Porcelain absorbs 0.5% of its weight or less, and a glaze seals the face.[15] Only a film on soap scum at the surface, which wipes off. Normal cleaning. A tiled floor lasts 75–100 years.[39]
Cement grout (EN 13888 class CG1 or CG2) Yes. The test allows standard CG1 grout to take up 5 g of water in 30 minutes and 10 g in 4 hours; water-repellent CG2 W grout 2 g and 5 g.[16] Grows into the pores, feeds on soap and skin oils, and stains the joint grey to black. Seal every 1–2 years.[27] Regrout every 8–15 years.[51]
Epoxy grout (class RG) Practically none. Stays on the surface and cleans off.[38] No sealing. Outlasts the bathroom.
Sanitary silicone At the surface, and horizontal joints stay wet after every shower. Black yeasts such as Aureobasidium and Exophiala push dark pigment into the silicone, and the stain can’t be cleaned out.[17][18] Replace every 1–2 years in a steamy room, 5–10 years with anti-fungal silicone and good ventilation.[18]

The tile size decides how much grout there is

Each square below is one square metre of wall, drawn to scale. Small tiles carry many times more joint, and every metre of joint is a metre that needs spraying, scrubbing, sealing and eventually regrouting.

Joint length per m² = 1 ÷ (tile width + joint) + 1 ÷ (tile height + joint). Joints are 2 mm wide, 3 mm for 30×60 cm. Metro tiles are drawn in the usual brick bond.

How fast it comes back

Mould on grout is a cycle. Cleaning removes what you can see, the joint stays damp, and growth starts again.

24–48 hours
Spores germinate on a surface that stays wet.[12]
1–4 weeks
Visible regrowth on grout and silicone in a shower with no working ventilation, according to bathroom trade guides.[19] The model’s poor-ventilation case assumes a treatment every two weeks.
1–2 years
Grout sealer stops beading water in a shower.[27] Silicone that was badly applied, or sits in a room that never dries, can already need replacing.[18][51]
5–10 years
Anti-fungal silicone lasts this long when the room dries out between showers.[18]
8–15 years
Cement grout has to be raked out and replaced.[51]
16–20 years
A typical bathroom gets renovated. German bathrooms that were never renovated average 19.5 years old, and across Europe at least 16 years pass between renovations.[41][42]
50+ years
Glazed tile would still be fine.[39] In practice it is torn out for style long before it wears out, so its long life rarely pays off.

The chemical bill

Most bathroom mould sprays are chlorine bleach: sodium hypochlorite at 1–5% in Tilex in the US, and about 2.2–2.6% in Cillit Bang Black Mould in the UK.[20][22] A 946 ml bottle of Tilex costs $5.48[21], 750 ml of Cillit Bang £4.50[22], and German drugstores sell 500–750 ml for €2.95–11.99.[23] That is roughly €4–24 per litre.

How much a household uses depends on how much grout there is and how often the mould returns. Product labels assume heavy use: 100–200 ml per m².[24][25] A trigger sprayer delivers 0.7–1.5 ml per squeeze[26], so misting a 10 cm band along each joint takes about 40 ml per m² of that band. The model uses this lighter figure, applied to the joints and silicone of a 6 m² shower wall.

Over 20 years, one tiled shower with classic tiles and average ventilation gets through:

  • 60 litres of chlorine spray, about 80 bottles, containing 1.6 kg of sodium hypochlorite
  • 19 coats of grout sealer, about 4.75 litres
  • 3 silicone renewals and 1 full regrout
  • 109 hours of spraying, scrubbing and sealing

Never combine chlorine spray with acidic cleaners such as limescale removers, many toilet cleaners or vinegar. The mix releases chlorine gas. US poison centres recorded 4,800 exposures from exactly this mix in 2021.[46]

What changes the answer

The table uses your calculator settings and varies only the ventilation. Darker cells cost more.

Wall optionPoor ventilationAverageGood
  1. Ventilation matters more than the wall material. With EU prices and your time counted, small tiles with cement grout cost €7,941 over 20 years in a badly ventilated room and €2,336 in a well ventilated one. The well-ventilated tiled shower beats wall panels in a badly ventilated room (€3,470).
  2. Epoxy grout pays for itself quickly. It adds about €90 to a 6 m² shower wall and earns that back within 2 years with your time counted, or 5 years in cash alone, at average ventilation.
  3. Panels stay cheaper in cash unless they are expensive. At average ventilation, tiles only win on cash if panels cost more than about €290 per m² fitted in Germany, £235 in the UK or $527 in the US ($49 per sq ft).
  4. Over 30 years the ranking shifts. Panels need replacing once, and tiles with epoxy grout become the cheapest option in cash: €2,104 against €2,197 for panels in the EU and $3,137 against $3,520 in the US. In the UK the two are level (£1,821 and £1,805).
  5. Chemicals never decide it. Across all three countries and ventilation levels, mould spray is about 3–13% of the tiled shower’s 20-year cost with time counted, and 4–26% of the cash spent.

Kitchens are a different case

A kitchen splashback gets steam in short bursts while you cook, then dries out. Grease is the main soiling, and mould on splashback grout is uncommon. Running the same model for a 2.5 m² tiled splashback with a cooker hood and two mould treatments a year gives about 0.2 litres of spray a year, under €2. Upkeep including time comes to about €19 a year, against €11 for a panel splashback. Over 20 years the difference between materials is mostly what they cost to fit.

Mould problems in kitchens usually start with leaks. In Sweden, appliances such as fridges, freezers and dishwashers cause about a quarter of reported water damage.[45] One study found the black yeast Exophiala dermatitidis on the rubber seals of 47% of dishwashers tested.[17] The places to watch are the silicone along the sink and worktop, the cabinet under the sink and the space behind the dishwasher.

What to do, best payback first

  1. Fix the ventilation. Run the fan during the shower and for 20 minutes after, or fit one with a humidity sensor, and squeegee the walls and glass, which takes about a minute. We assume a fitted fan costs €300, £250 or $400, plus €10–15 a year to run. Compared with a badly ventilated room it saves about €5,000, £5,000 or $9,300 on a tiled shower over 20 years. The running cost is a 15 W fan for an hour a day (about 5.5 kWh a year) plus the warm air it removes (about 50 kWh of heat a season), at the EU average of €0.29 per kWh.[50]
  2. Use epoxy grout in the shower when you renovate. It costs about €15 per m² more in Germany and $4–8 per sq ft more in the US.[31][38] Choose large tiles: 60×120 cm tiles have about a quarter of the joint length of 20×25 cm tiles.
  3. Or put grout-free panels in the shower zone only and keep tiles everywhere else. In the UK, the main panel makers now give 30-year warranties.[36]
  4. Use chlorine spray for spot treatment, not as a routine. Replace silicone once it is stained black instead of bleaching it every month.
  5. Treat mould that returns within weeks as a warning. It points to a ventilation or leak problem. Tenants in English social housing can hold landlords to the Awaab’s Law deadlines.[6]

Method and limits

The model covers the wet zone of one bathroom: 6 m² of wall, which is a 90×90 cm shower tiled to 2.2 m on three sides, or a US five-foot tub surround of about 60 sq ft. It adds up the installation (materials, labour, and the waterproofing membrane that tiles need), mould spray, grout sealer, silicone renewals, regrouting, replacement of panels that wear out, and the hours spent. Hours are valued at a cleaner’s hourly pay: €17 in Germany, £18 in the UK and $35 in the US.[47][48][49]

Assumptions you can change in the calculator: 26, 12 or 4 mould treatments a year for poor, average and good ventilation; silicone lasting 2, 5 or 8 years; cement grout lasting 8, 12 or 16 years and resealed every 1, 1 or 2 years; 40 ml of spray per m² over a 10 cm band along each joint; scrubbing at 3 metres of joint a minute plus 5 minutes to set up. Epoxy joints need spraying half as often and scrub twice as fast. Panels have 11 m of silicone seams against 9 m for tiles.

Left out: weekly cleaning, which any surface needs; the energy cost of ventilation, except in the fan payback; health costs; resale value; and looks.

Water damage is also left out, because it depends on the waterproofing behind the wall rather than on the surface. It is still the biggest financial risk in a wet room. A US water damage claim averages about $15,400.[43] German insurers paid €4.9 billion for more than a million burst-pipe and leak claims in 2024.[44] In Sweden, 24% of water damage happens in bathrooms, and 12% is traced to failed waterproofing in wet rooms.[45] Grout is not waterproof, so a tiled shower is only as sound as the membrane behind it.

Industry life-cycle studies rank ceramic tile as the cheapest floor covering over 50 years, at about $0.33 per sq ft per year.[40] Those studies cover floors, not shower walls, and leave out mould upkeep.
